NGSS lesson plans built around narrated tours

Three ready-to-teach lessons for grades 3–12. Each one pairs a narrated Solaris tour with a hands-on activity, discussion prompts and an assessment. Print them, or embed this page in your LMS.

How to run a tour in class

1. Open the link

Each lesson has a tour link such as /explorer.html?tour=inner-planets. Project it, or share it so students follow along on their own devices. On slow school Wi-Fi add &lite=1.

2. Press Start

The tour begins after the splash screen. Narration uses the browser’s built-in voice, so nothing is installed. Captions always appear beneath the scene for students who are deaf or hard of hearing.

3. Pause and probe

Use the pause button at any stop. The object panel shows real figures next to the compressed model — the gap between them is the teaching moment in every lesson below.

The lessons

Lesson 1 · Grades 3–5 · 45 min

The Inner Planets

5-ESS1-2 3-5-ETS1-2 Tour: 4 min

Students compare the four rocky worlds, notice patterns in size, distance and temperature, and build an argument for why Earth is the one with liquid water.

Learning objectives

  • Describe the order of the inner planets from the Sun and identify one distinguishing feature of each.
  • Use data from the object panels to explain the pattern between distance from the Sun and surface temperature.
  • Argue from evidence why Earth is in the “habitable zone”.

Sequence

  1. Engage (5 min) — Ask: “If you could stand on any planet, which would be hottest? Why?” Collect predictions on the board.
  2. Explore (10 min) — Run the Inner Planets tour. Students fill in the first three columns of the data table (distance, temperature, moons) as each stop is narrated.
  3. Explain (10 min) — Pause on Venus. Compare Venus and Earth: almost the same size, very different temperature. Introduce the idea of an atmosphere trapping heat.
  4. Elaborate (15 min) — Pairs open Mars and Mercury from the search bar and complete the “Would I need…?” design sheet (heat shield, oxygen, water, sunscreen).
  5. Evaluate (5 min) — Exit ticket: “Earth has liquid water because …” using at least one number from the table.

NGSS alignment

5-ESS1-2 Represent data in graphical displays to reveal patterns of daily changes and seasonal appearance of some stars in the night sky. (Extended here to patterns across the inner planets.)
3-5-ETS1-2 Generate and compare multiple possible solutions to a problem based on how well each is likely to meet the criteria and constraints.

Materials

  • Projector or 1:1 devices with the tour link
  • Printed data table (four rows: Mercury, Venus, Earth, Mars)
  • “Would I need…?” design sheet

Differentiation

  • Pre-fill the Mercury row for students who need a model.
  • Extension: add the Moon to the table and ask why it is so different from Earth despite the same distance from the Sun.

Assessment

Exit ticket rubric: names the habitable zone or distance (1), cites a temperature or distance figure (1), links atmosphere to temperature (1).

Lesson 2 · Grades 6–8 · 50 min

Giants of the Outer System

MS-ESS1-2 MS-ESS1-3 Tour: 5 min

Students analyse scale properties of the giant planets and their moons, then develop a model explaining how gravity shaped the outer solar system.

Learning objectives

  • Compare diameter and mass of Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune relative to Earth using the ratio readouts in the stats table.
  • Explain how gravity drives tidal heating on Io and holds Saturn’s rings and moons in orbit.
  • Construct a model that accounts for why giants formed far from the Sun.

Sequence

  1. Engage (5 min) — Show the overview with the Orbits layer on. Ask why the outer planets move so slowly compared with Mercury.
  2. Explore (12 min) — Run the Gas Giants tour. Students record the Earth-relative diameter and mass (the × readouts) at each stop.
  3. Explain (10 min) — Pause on Io. Model tidal flexing with a stress ball squeezed rhythmically — where does the heat come from?
  4. Elaborate (18 min) — Groups build a “frost line” model: why could ice survive beyond Jupiter and not inside Mars? Groups present a one-slide explanation with a sketch.
  5. Evaluate (5 min) — Two-question quiz: rank the giants by mass; explain in one sentence why Saturn is less dense than water.

NGSS alignment

MS-ESS1-2 Develop and use a model to describe the role of gravity in the motions within galaxies and the solar system.
MS-ESS1-3 Analyze and interpret data to determine scale properties of objects in the solar system.

Materials

  • Projector with the tour link; Orbits layer enabled
  • Ratio recording sheet (diameter ×, mass ×, moons)
  • One stress ball per table group

Differentiation

  • Provide the Jupiter row as a worked example.
  • Extension: open Today in space and relate the current Kp index to Jupiter’s far stronger magnetic field.

Assessment

Model presentation rubric: identifies gravity as the organising force (2), uses at least two data points from the tour (2), explains the frost line (1).

Lesson 3 · Grades 9–12 · 55 min

The Scale of Space

HS-ESS1-4 HS-PS2-4 Tour: 4 min

Students use Kepler’s third law to test the model, quantify exactly how Solaris compresses distance, and reason about light-travel time to the nearest stars.

Learning objectives

  • Verify Kepler’s third law (T2 ∝ a3) using the orbital period and distance figures from the object panels.
  • Calculate the compression factor the model applies to Neptune versus Earth and explain why a faithful model would be unusable.
  • Convert light-years to travel time and relate the space-weather halo to the solar wind reaching Earth.

Sequence

  1. Engage (5 min) — Show the “marble Earth, beach-ball Sun 150 m away” analogy from the tour’s first stop. Ask students to estimate where Neptune would be on campus.
  2. Explore (10 min) — Run the Scale of Space tour with the space-weather layer on. Students copy distance (AU) and period (days) for Earth, Mars, Jupiter and Neptune.
  3. Explain (15 min) — Compute T2 / a3 for each planet. Discuss why the ratio is (nearly) constant and what deviation would imply.
  4. Elaborate (20 min) — Using the model’s displayed distances, students derive the non-linear compression Solaris uses and plot real vs. model distance. Then: how long has the light from Sirius and Betelgeuse been travelling?
  5. Evaluate (5 min) — Written response: “Is Solaris a good model? Cite one thing it gets right and one shortcut, with numbers.”

NGSS alignment

HS-ESS1-4 Use mathematical or computational representations to predict the motion of orbiting objects in the solar system.
HS-PS2-4 Use mathematical representations of Newton’s Law of Gravitation to describe and predict the gravitational forces between objects.

Materials

  • 1:1 devices with the tour link
  • Calculators or a spreadsheet
  • Graph paper or plotting tool for real vs. model distance

Differentiation

  • Provide the Earth row pre-computed as the reference (T2/a3 = 1 in Earth units).
  • Extension: check the current solar wind speed on Today and compute how long a gust takes to cross 1 AU.

Assessment

Response rubric: correct Kepler ratio to two significant figures (2), correct light-travel reasoning (1), model critique cites both a real figure and a compressed figure (2).
