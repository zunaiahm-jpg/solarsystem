About

A semi-realistic solar system, honest about its shortcuts

Solaris is an interactive model of the solar system that is quite, but not entirely, realistic. This page explains what is real, what is compressed, and exactly where every byte of data comes from.

Why compress space at all?

At true scale, if Earth were a marble, the Sun would be a beach ball 150 metres away and Neptune would be four and a half kilometres from here. A faithful model is mostly empty black, which is accurate but not much use in a classroom.

Solaris keeps the parts that matter for understanding — relative sizes within a family of objects, orbital periods, axial tilts, inclinations, the direction everything moves — and algorithmically shrinks distances and time so the whole system stays in view and visibly alive. Every object panel shows the real figures next to the model, and the lesson plans lean into that gap as a teaching moment.

What is real

  • Orbital periods, tilts and inclinations for the Sun, eight planets and the major moons, seeded to their J2000 heliocentric longitudes so planets start in roughly the right place for today’s date.
  • Surface imagery — every textured body wears an 8K map built from NASA mission data (see sources below).
  • Earth’s clouds are refreshed daily from real satellite imagery.
  • The sky is a photographic all-sky plate, not procedural noise; named stars and constellations are placed from their catalogue positions.
  • Live layers — the ISS position, near-Earth asteroid passes and solar activity come straight from public NASA and NOAA feeds while you watch.
  • Every statistic in the object panels (diameter, mass, day length, year length, moon counts) is a published figure.

What is compressed or schematic

  • Distances between planets are shrunk non-linearly so the outer system is reachable.
  • Sizes are exaggerated relative to orbital distance so planets are visible from the overview.
  • Time runs faster than real time; the slider goes from paused to 20×.
  • The ISS orbits at about 420 km, which would sit inside Earth’s pixel at this scale. Its marker is drawn at a readable altitude; the latitude, longitude, altitude and speed shown are the real numbers.
  • Asteroid positions are placed schematically around Earth using the logarithm of their real miss distance. The distance, speed and size figures on each marker are real.
  • The space-weather halo on the Sun is a visual cue driven by the current GOES X-ray flare class and Kp index. It is not a rendering of the corona.

Technology

Rendering

Three.js (WebGL 2) with a physically based sky shader, ACES tone mapping and a single bloom pass. No film grain: real space has none.

Immersive VR

WebXR with a comfort camera rig, thumbstick flight, snap-turn and a wrist HUD. Works in Quest Browser and any WebXR-capable headset without installing anything.

Narrated tours

Built on the browser’s Web Speech API, so there are no downloads, accounts or per-seat licences. Captions are always shown for accessibility and for browsers without speech.

Low-bandwidth mode

Swaps the ~70 MB texture set for ~5 MB, caps the sky at 4K and drops post-processing. Turns on automatically with Data Saver, or via the settings panel or ?lite=1.

Privacy

No analytics, no trackers, no cookies. Videos use YouTube’s privacy-enhanced domain. The only data stored locally is your low-bandwidth preference.

Accessibility

Every control is keyboard reachable with visible focus, live regions announce feed updates and tour captions, and the site respects reduced-motion preferences.
